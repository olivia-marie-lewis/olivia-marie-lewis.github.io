I first became interested in the connection between transportation planning and public health while working as an Assistant Researcher at Tufts University School of Medicine. I was doing many analyses of spatial access to treatment for opioid use disorder, looking at metrics like walk and drive-times. This work sparked my interest in how land use and transportation planning affects health by influencing the spatial distribution of important resources, like jobs and healthcare, and people's ability to reach them. I became interested in how transportation systems can expand access to healthcare by improving underlying infrastructure in addition to establishing new facilities.  

My interest in transportation and health was reinforced by beginning to bike around my community. Throughout college, I frequently used public transit, but I had never before ridden a bike. After I learned and began riding more around my city, I was struck by the realities of urban cycling: riding alongside fast-moving traffic, maneuvering around cars parked in bike lanes, and bouncing over potholes. These experiences showed me that the ability to bike safely and comfortably depends not only on individual choice, but also on the infrastructure surrounding it. I came to see transportation planning as a way to practice public health: by creating communities where safe, sustainable, and accessible transportation options are available to everyone. This ultimately led me to pursue my Master's degree at UC Berkeley, which will prepare me to work towards expanding access to safe, sustainable modes of transportation.
